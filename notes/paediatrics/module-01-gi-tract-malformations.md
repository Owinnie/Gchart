## General Causes and Overview

- Malformations typically occur during the organogenesis period (the first 8–12 weeks of gestation). ^(DOCX)
- The majority (50%) have an unknown cause. ^(DOCX)
- Other associations include genetic links (gene defects, chromosomal abnormalities, multifactorial inheritance), environmental teratogens (drugs, alcohol, tobacco, chemicals, radiation, or infections like rubella and CMV), and micronutrient deficiencies (like folic acid). ^(DOCX)
- The text outlines malformations across the esophagus (Esophageal atresia, Tracheo-esophageal fistula), stomach (Hypertrophic pyloric stenosis, gastric volvulus, pyloric atresia), small intestines (Duodenal or Ileal atresia/stenosis), large intestines (Meckel's diverticulum, Hirchsprung's disease), and anorectal malformations. ^(DOCX)

## 1. Esophageal Atresia (EA) and Tracheo-esophageal Fistula (TEF)

- **Overview:** EA is a birth disorder where the esophagus fails to develop properly, affecting about 1 in 4,000 neonates. Over 90% of these cases have an associated TEF. ^(DOCX +1)
- **Most Common Form:** The upper esophagus ends in a blind pouch, and the TEF connects to the distal esophagus. ^(DOCX)
- **Risks & Associations:** Infants weighing less than 1,500 g at birth face the highest mortality risk. 50% of infants have associated anomalies, notably VATER/VACTERL syndrome (Vertebral, Anorectal, Trachea, Esophagus, Cardiac, Renal, Radial, and Limb). ^(DOCX +1)
- **Clinical Presentation:** Neonates typically exhibit frothing and bubbling at the mouth and nose, respiratory distress, and episodes of coughing and cyanosis. Feeding makes symptoms worse (regurgitation and aspiration). Aspiration of gastric contents causes pneumonitis, and patients may suffer from chronic respiratory problems like refractory bronchospasm and recurrent pneumonias. ^(DOCX +2)
- **Diagnosis:** Early-onset respiratory distress paired with the inability to pass a nasogastric or orogastric tube is highly suggestive. Plain radiography may show a coiled feeding tube in the pouch and an air-distended stomach (indicating a coexisting TEF). Pure EA may present as an airless, scaphoid abdomen. Prenatally, maternal polyhydramnios is a warning sign. Further tests include an esophagogram (using contrast) or bronchoscopy (using methylene blue dye injected into the endotracheal tube). ^(DOCX +4)
- **Management:** The priority is maintaining a patent airway and preventing aspiration. Prone positioning helps minimize the movement of gastric secretions into a distal fistula, while continuous suctioning clears the blind pouch. Endotracheal intubation with ambu bagging or a ventilator should be avoided as it worsens abdominal viscera distention. Surgical ligation of the TEF and primary end-to-end anastomosis of the esophagus are the definitive treatments, alongside a careful search for cardiac anomalies. ^(DOCX +3)
- **Outcomes & Complications:** Most infants grow up to lead normal lives. Surgical complications include anastomotic leak, re-fistulization, anastomotic stricture, and Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease (GERD). ^(DOCX +1)

## 2. Gastric Outlet Obstruction

This category includes hypertrophic pyloric stenosis, pyloric atresia, and gastric volvulus. ^(DOCX)

### Infantile Hypertrophic Pyloric Stenosis

- **Overview:** Caused by hypertrophy and hyperplasia of the pylorus muscle layers. The prevalence in the USA is roughly 3 in 1,000 infants. ^(DOCX +1)
- **Demographics & Genetics:** It is most common in whites of northern European ancestry, less common in blacks, and rare in Asians. Males (especially firstborns) are affected about four times as often as females. There is a 20% inheritance risk for male descendants (and 10% for female descendants) of a mother who had it. ^(DOCX +2)
- **Associations:** Higher incidence in infants with type B and O blood groups. Associated with other defects like TEF. ^(DOCX +1)
- **Etiology:** The exact cause is unknown, but it is highly concordant in monozygotic twins. Implicated factors include abnormal muscle innervation, elevated prostaglandins, reduced pyloric nitric oxide synthase, infant hypergastrinemia, and eosinophilic gastroenteritis. It is also associated with syndromes (Apert, Zellweger, trisomy 18, Smith-Lemli-Opitz) and the use of erythromycin in neonates. ^(DOCX +2)
- **Clinical Presentation:** Symptoms usually do not present at birth. Non-bilious vomiting typically starts after 3 weeks of age (ranging from the 1st week to the 5th month). Vomiting occurs immediately after a feeding, is progressive (may become projectile), and the infant remains hungry afterward. ^(DOCX +2)
- **Metabolic Effects:** Continuous vomiting leads to a loss of fluid, hydrogen ions, and chloride, causing hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis. Serum potassium is usually maintained, though total body potassium drops. Jaundice is seen in about 5% of cases. ^(DOCX +2)
- **Diagnosis:** Clinically diagnosed in 60-80% of cases by palpating a firm, movable, olive-shaped mass (~2 cm) in the mid epigastrium beneath the liver edge, which is easier to feel after the infant vomits. A visible gastric peristaltic wave may progress across the abdomen after feeding. Ultrasound confirms the diagnosis (95% sensitivity) if pyloric thickness is > 4 mm or length is > 14 mm. Barium studies reveal an elongated channel, a "shoulder sign" (bulge into the antrum), and a "double tract sign" (parallel barium streaks). ^(DOCX +3)
- **Differential Diagnosis:** Includes overly reactive infants, GERD, adrenal insufficiency (which presents with acidosis and elevated potassium, unlike pyloric stenosis), inborn errors of metabolism, gastroenteritis, duodenal stenosis, and rare pyloric duplication. ^(DOCX)
- **Treatment:** Medical therapy focuses on correcting dehydration and acid-base/electrolyte imbalances (using 0.45-0.9% saline in 5-10% dextrose with potassium chloride) until serum bicarbonate is < 30 mEq/dL. The surgical treatment of choice is the Ramstedt pyloromyotomy (splitting the muscle without cutting the mucosa). Feedings resume 12-24 hours post-surgery. Operative mortality is 0-0.5%, and endoscopic balloon dilation is an option for persistent vomiting secondary to incomplete pyloromyotomy. ^(DOCX +3)

### Congenital Gastric Outlet Obstruction

- **Overview:** Results from pyloric atresia and antral webs, making up less than 1% of all atresias. Equal sex distribution, and associated with epidermolysis bullosa. ^(DOCX +1)
- **Clinical Presentation:** Infants present on the 1st day of life with non-bilious vomiting, feeding difficulties, abdominal distention, and low birth weight. Polyhydramnios is common. The stomach may rupture as early as the first 12 hours of life. ^(DOCX +2)
- **Diagnosis & Treatment:** Diagnosed by a large, dilated stomach on plain radiographs and a "pyloric dimple" on contrast series. Treatment involves correcting dehydration/alkalosis, nasogastric decompression, and surgical/endoscopic repair once the patient is stable. ^(DOCX +1)

## 3. Duodenal Obstruction – Atresia

- **Overview:** Forms include an intact obstructing membrane (most common), a short fibrous cord connecting two blind pouches, or a complete gap between duodenal ends. Obstruction can also result from extrinsic compression (annular pancreas or Ladd bands in malrotation). ^(DOCX +1)
- **Associations:** Down syndrome occurs in 20-30% of cases. Other anomalies include malrotation (20%), esophageal atresia (10-20%), congenital heart disease (10-15%), and anorectal/renal anomalies (5%). ^(DOCX +1)
- **Clinical Presentation:** The hallmark is bilious vomiting without abdominal distention on the 1st day of life. Polyhydramnios is present in 50% of pregnancies, and jaundice in 33% of infants. Early peristaltic waves may be seen. ^(DOCX +2)
- **Diagnosis & Treatment:** Prenatal ultrasound can detect it, and abdominal x-rays show a classic "double-bubble sign". Initial treatment is NG/OG decompression and IV fluids. An echocardiogram and spine x-ray should be done to check for life-threatening anomalies. Definitive treatment is a duodenoduodenostomy. ^(DOCX +3)

## 4. Meckel Diverticulum

- **Overview:** A remnant of the embryonic yolk sac (omphalomesenteric or vitelline duct). Normally, this duct attenuates between the 5th and 7th week of gestation. The diverticulum is a 3-6 cm outpouching of the ileum on the antimesenteric border, located 50-75 cm from the ileocecal valve. ^(DOCX +2)
- **Clinical Presentation:** Symptoms usually arise in the first 2 years or 1st decade of life. The diverticulum is often lined by ectopic acid-secreting mucosa, causing ulceration of adjacent normal ileum. This presents as intermittent, painless rectal bleeding (described as brick-colored or currant jelly). It may cause anemia or bowel obstruction, but bleeding is often self-limiting due to splanchnic vessel contraction as hypovolemia sets in. ^(DOCX +3)
- **Diagnosis & Treatment:** The most sensitive diagnostic study is a Meckel radionuclide scan (technetium-99m pertechnetate). Treatment for symptomatic cases is surgical excision. ^(DOCX +1)

## 5. Congenital Aganglionic Megacolon (Hirschsprung's Disease)

- **Overview:** Caused by abnormal bowel innervation due to an arrest of neuroblast migration from the proximal to distal bowel. This leaves an absence of ganglion cells (Meissner and Auerbach plexus) starting at the internal anal sphincter and extending proximally. ^(DOCX +1)
- **Demographics & Associations:** Most common cause of lower intestinal obstruction in neonates (1 in 5,000 live births). Males are affected more (4:1). Associated defects include Down syndrome, Smith-Lemli-Opitz, cartilage-hair hypoplasia, congenital hypoventilation ("Ondine curse"), and urogenital/cardiovascular abnormalities. ^(DOCX +2)
- **Pathology:** The aganglionic segment is limited to the rectosigmoid in 75% of patients, though 10% lack ganglion cells in the entire colon. Histology reveals hypertrophied nerve bundles with high concentrations of acetylcholinesterase. ^(DOCX +1)
- **Clinical Presentation:** Symptoms begin at birth with delayed passage of meconium, followed by chronic constipation, failure to thrive (with hypoproteinemia), and abdominal distention. Bowel dilation increases intraluminal pressure, decreasing blood flow and allowing bacterial proliferation, which can lead to enterocolitis and sepsis. ^(DOCX +1)
- **Older Patients:** Intermittent intestinal obstruction pain/fever. A large fecal mass is palpable in the left lower abdomen, but the rectum is paradoxically empty on examination. The digital rectal exam usually triggers an explosive discharge of foul-smelling feces and gas. ^(DOCX +2)
- **Diagnosis & Treatment:** Diagnosed via rectal manometry, plain x-rays, barium enema (showing a "rat tail" narrow aganglionic segment below a distended normal colon), and confirmed by rectal suction biopsy. Treatment is surgical: excising the aganglionic segment and anastomosing the normal bowel to the rectum, either immediately or following a temporary colostomy. ^(DOCX +1)

## 6. Anorectal Malformations

- **Overview:** Complex congenital anomalies involving the anus, rectum, and often the urinary/genital tracts. Incidence is 2-2.5 per 10,000 live births, with a 1.5:1 male-to-female ratio. Supra-levator lesions and associated anomalies are more common in males. ^(DOCX +2)
- **Most Common Forms:** Recto-urethral fistula in males and ano-vestibular fistula in females. ^(DOCX)
- **Risk Factors:** Mothers >35 years old, maternal recreational drug use, high altitude, consanguinity, paternal exposure to vehicle manufacturing teratogens, and raw vegetable (pesticide) consumption. ^(DOCX)
- **Diagnosis & Treatment:** Prenatal ultrasound has low sensitivity but may show a dilated colon, oligohydramnios/highly distended vagina (in females), absent anal hypoechogenicity, or enterolithiasis. Plain x-rays and an invertogram are used postnatally. Management involves pediatric surgery (anoplasty, anorectoplasty, and fistula repair). ^(DOCX +2)
